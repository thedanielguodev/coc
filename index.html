Fire → homelessness correlation

—
Each point is one CoC in one year: acres burned within that CoC's boundary (x) vs. its homeless count (y). Points at x = 0 are CoC-years with no major (≥1,000-acre) fire on record. Line is the least-squares fit. Default timing is fire-year → next year's count (t → t+1), on the theory that displacement shows up in the following PIT count rather than the same one; toggle to "Same year" to compare. Large urban CoCs dominate homeless totals but rarely burn heavily, while large rural CoCs burn heavily but have small populations — this population/geography mismatch pulls the raw correlation toward zero independent of any real relationship, so treat r as a starting point, not a verdict.

Fire → homelessness, year by year

—
The pooled chart above averages all 18 years together, which could hide a real effect that only shows up in specific catastrophic-fire years. This instead computes a separate cross-sectional correlation within each single year, across all 44 CoCs. Orange bars pass an uncorrected p < 0.05; solid orange survives correcting for testing 18 years at once. Hover any bar for its leave-one-out check (does the result survive dropping that year's single largest-acreage CoC?). Generated by compute_yearly_fire_influence.py.

AI vs. ground truth

—

Each point is one CoC, colored by the AI's own stated confidence for that estimate — click a point (or a table row below) to select that CoC on the map. X is the real per-CoC Pearson r (same-year lag) between fire acreage and homeless count, computed directly from the joined data. Y is an LLM's estimate of that same figure, made from that CoC's wildfire history alone — it was never shown the actual homeless counts. The gray dashed line is y = x (what perfect agreement would look like); the solid orange line is the actual least-squares trend through the AI's own guesses — how flat it is versus the diagonal shows the AI's guesses barely track the real value at all, not just that they're shifted upward. Generated by ai_estimate_influence.py (needs an ANTHROPIC_API_KEY) or a blind Claude Code sub-agent given only fire histories, then compare_ai_vs_ground_truth.py; this panel is blank until those have been run. Case study: Paradise, Butte County

—
The 2018 Camp Fire destroyed the town of Paradise almost entirely — the single most catastrophic housing-loss wildfire event in this dataset. Even here, Butte County's official PIT homeless count barely moves relative to its own noisy year-to-year swings. This is why the pooled statewide null result above isn't a data artifact: it holds up even in the most extreme real case, because HUD's PIT count only captures people literally unsheltered or in emergency shelter on one specific night — it structurally excludes most disaster-displaced people (doubled up with family, in FEMA housing, or relocated out of county).
