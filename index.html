Do wildfires show up in California's homeless counts?

Eighteen years of federal homeless counts for California's 44 Continuums of Care, compared with state wildfire records, and with estimates from an AI model that never saw the counts.

Daniel Guo · Working draft, updated September 30, 2026 · Code and data on GitHub

Summary

A Continuum of Care (CoC) is the local body that runs HUD's annual Point-in-Time (PIT) count of people experiencing homelessness. This project matches each CoC's yearly count to the wildfires that burned inside its boundary, then asks whether regions with more fire damage count more homeless residents.

  • Pooled across 712 CoC-years, acres burned and the homeless count are essentially uncorrelated (Pearson r = 0.027). Counts of structures destroyed, available from 2013, give the same result (r = 0.003). The 2021 count is left out of every statistic because most California CoCs skipped the unsheltered count that year.
  • When each region is compared only with its own history, years with larger fire losses have slightly higher counts. The only estimate that reaches significance (p = 0.023) depends entirely on Butte County: without it, p = 0.94.
  • The observed relationship does not differ across regions by more than sampling noise, so no census or insurance measure explains where it is stronger.
  • An AI model given only each region's fire history predicted a positive relationship in all 440 of its estimates. On average it overstated the observed correlation by 0.22.
  • The AI's estimate for a region rises with the number of structures its fires destroyed (ρ = 0.80). The observed correlation does not (ρ = −0.13). The AI is therefore most wrong where fires were most destructive.
  • The model's errors were largest when it described itself as highly confident.
  • Told how the PIT count is collected, the model lowered its estimates but still overstated the correlation. A second model, Gemini, overstated it much less. Across all 3,080 estimates from both models and every prompt, only one was negative, and neither model could tell which regions had a stronger relationship.
  • Removing the prompt's list of housing-loss mechanisms and its instruction not to "hedge toward zero" lowered the model's estimate in every region (by 0.06 on average) and cut its overstatement roughly in half. Gemini's neutral estimates matched the data on average. Both models' estimates still rose with the number of structures destroyed.

The PIT count misses many people displaced by fire, such as families staying with relatives or in hotels paid by insurance or FEMA. A weak result here does not show that wildfires have no effect on housing loss. A national study of all 50 states found that homes lost to climate disasters were associated with larger increases in homelessness (Leifheit et al., 2026). The AI's positive direction may be right. This page measures how well it estimates the size of the relationship that appears in HUD's CoC-level counts.

Fire and homeless counts

Each dot in Figure 2 is one CoC in one year. If fires pushed people into homelessness in the same region, dots further right would tend to sit higher. They do not. Large urban CoCs have the highest counts and rarely burn, while rural CoCs burn the most and have small populations.

AI estimates

An AI model (Claude) received each CoC's name and its year-by-year acres burned, with no homeless counts, and was asked to estimate the correlation between the two. The experiment was run 10 separate times with no memory between runs. Figure 4 compares the average estimate for each CoC with the value computed from the data.

One possible explanation is that the model leans on well-known recent fires. If so, CoCs whose worst fire was recent should have larger errors. Figure 6 shows no such pattern. What does predict the error is how destructive the fires were, not how recent (see Where the AI is most wrong).

Does knowing how the data is collected change the estimates?

Part of the gap could come from the model not knowing how the PIT count works. To test this, the experiment was repeated with one added paragraph describing what the count includes and leaves out. It was also run on a second model, Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, with both versions of the prompt. Each combination had 10 independent runs.

The original prompt also lists ways fires can cause housing loss and asks for an estimate that is "not a hedge toward zero." To test whether that wording inflates the estimates, a neutral version removes exactly those two phrases and changes nothing else. Because the Claude model available on September 30 answered the original prompt differently from the one used earlier, the neutral runs were compared with 10 runs of the original prompt made in the same batch.

Where the AI is most wrong

The gap between the AI's estimate and the observed correlation varies a lot from region to region. That variation can come from either side. Each region's observed r rests on at most 17 years of counts, so it is noisy. The AI's estimate, averaged over 10 runs, is not: the two halves of its runs agree almost perfectly (split-half reliability 0.98).

Table 5 tests whether the observed correlations differ across regions by more than their sampling noise. They do not, once 2021 is handled. So a regional difference in the AI's error mostly reflects what the AI believes about that region, and the useful question is what drives its estimate. Figure 8 shows the answer: how destructive the region's fires were.

Butte County and the Camp Fire

The 2018 Camp Fire destroyed most of the town of Paradise in Butte County, roughly 14,000 to 19,000 structures. It is the largest loss of housing in this dataset.

Butte also drives the one significant fixed-effects result for structures destroyed (Table 2): the same-year estimate has p = 0.023 with Butte included and p = 0.94 without it. That result is not the Camp Fire raising the count. Same-year timing pairs the November 2018 fire with the January 2018 count, taken before the fire, and with next-year timing the model is not significant. Butte is also where the AI gave its highest estimate (0.72).

Data and methods

Data

  • Homeless counts: HUD Point-in-Time counts by CoC, 2007–2024, from HUD User.
  • CoC boundaries: HUD eGIS Open Data.
  • Fire perimeters: CAL FIRE historical fire perimeters, limited to fires of at least 1,000 acres from 2000 on.
  • Structures destroyed: CAL FIRE Damage Inspection (DINS), 2013 on.
  • State outline, used to clip fires and boundaries to California: Census TIGERweb.
  • Demographics: American Community Survey 2019–2023 five-year estimates by census tract.
  • Insurance: California Department of Insurance new, renewed, and nonrenewed homeowners policies by ZIP code, 2015–2021.

Matching fires to regions

Each fire perimeter is intersected with every CoC boundary. When a fire crosses a boundary, its acres are divided in proportion to the area on each side. Each destroyed structure is assigned to the CoC that contains it. Census tracts and insurance ZIP codes are assigned to the CoC containing their center point; where the Los Angeles boundary overlaps Pasadena and Glendale, the smaller CoC is used.

Statistics

The 2021 count is excluded from every statistic. HUD allowed CoCs to skip the unsheltered count that year because of COVID-19, and 36 of California's 44 CoCs reported no unsheltered people. Including it pairs the record 2020 fire season with an artificially low count. Pooled results use Pearson and Spearman correlations across all CoC-years. The yearly results use a Bonferroni correction for 17 tests and a leave-one-out check that drops the CoC with the most acres burned that year. The fixed-effects models include a separate intercept for every CoC and every year, with standard errors clustered by CoC, and are refit without the CoC that had the largest single fire year. Differences in the observed correlation across CoCs are tested with Cochran's Q on Fisher-transformed correlations. Relationships with census and insurance measures use Spearman correlations with a Benjamini-Hochberg correction, rank partial correlations that hold structures destroyed and population density fixed, and a precision-weighted meta-regression of the observed correlations.

AI experiment

The model saw each CoC's name, number, and acres burned by year. It never saw homeless counts or any of the results on this page. It returned an estimated correlation, a direction, a confidence level, and a short explanation for each CoC. All 44 CoCs were sent together in each of 10 independent runs. The estimate shown for a CoC is the mean across runs. The follow-up runs used the same prompt with one added paragraph describing the PIT count, and were repeated on Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite through the Gemini API. The exact prompts and every raw answer are in the data files below.

Limitations

  • The analysis is correlational and cannot establish cause in either direction.
  • PIT counts are a single-night snapshot, and counting methods differ across CoCs and years. Dropping 2021 leaves Nevada County with no usable fire years, so it is excluded from the per-CoC comparison.
  • Each CoC's observed correlation rests on at most 17 years, so single-CoC results are noisy. Statements about individual regions should be read with that in mind.
  • The PIT count is taken in January, before that year's fire season. The AI was asked about the same-year relationship, so that is the benchmark; next-year timing is reported alongside it.
  • Fires under 1,000 acres are excluded, and CoC boundaries are treated as fixed across all years.
  • Two AI models were tested, and all regions were estimated in the same request for each run.
  • The original prompt lists ways fires can cause housing loss and asks for an estimate that is "not a hedge toward zero." A neutral version without that wording lowered Claude's estimates by about 0.06 and cut its overstatement roughly in half, so part of the original overstatement comes from the prompt. On Gemini, the neutral prompt brought the average estimate down to the observed average, but its estimates still rose with structures destroyed.
